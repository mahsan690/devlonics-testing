Before-and-after photos are the easiest marketing a contractor can do: they cost nothing, you're on the job anyway, and they show a homeowner exactly what you'll do for them. A cracked driveway next to a finished epoxy floor, a rusted water heater next to a new install, a weedy yard next to a fresh landscape. No headline is as convincing.

Here's where to use them, how to take them well and what to avoid.

## Where before-and-after photos earn their keep

**Your Google Business Profile.** Add real job photos regularly. They're one of the first things people see when they look at your listing, and they show you're active and local. Customers can also add their own photos and photo updates to your profile, and you can reply to them; Google notes these [don't affect your review score](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050?hl=en).

**Weekly Google posts.** A before-and-after with two lines about the job and the town is the simplest [Google Business Profile post](/blog/google-business-profile-posts) there is.

**Service pages.** Put two or three relevant pairs on each service page: drain jobs on the drain cleaning page, roof replacements on the roof replacement page.

**A project gallery.** A page of recent work, filterable by service or town, gives people browsing for bigger jobs something to look through.

**Quotes and proposals.** A few photos of similar jobs inside your estimate make the price easier to say yes to.

**Social media and Nextdoor.** Before-and-afters are the posts people actually stop on.

## How to take good before-and-after photos

1. **Shoot from the same spot.** Same angle, same height, same framing. Mark where you stood if it helps.
2. **Use good light.** Daylight, or turn on every light in the room. Avoid shooting into a bright window.
3. **Hold the phone level** and wipe the lens. It sounds obvious; it fixes half of bad photos.
4. **Show the whole area first,** then a close-up of the detail that matters.
5. **Shoot landscape for websites** and profiles, plus one portrait shot for stories and phones.
6. **Take more than you need.** Pick the best later.

Make it part of the job: the first thing a tech does on arrival and the last thing before leaving.

## What to leave out

- House numbers, street signs and addresses
- Faces, unless people have agreed
- License plates on driveways
- Personal items, family photos and paperwork inside the home

And ask the customer before you publish photos of their home. Many contractors add a line to their work order so it's agreed up front.

## Honesty rules

- **Only show your own work.** Never present stock photos or another company's work as jobs you did.
- **Don't edit away reality.** Brightness and cropping are fine; removing defects that are still there is not.
- **Match photos to services you offer** in the areas you serve.

Real photos are proof. Faked proof destroys the trust the rest of your marketing builds.

## Help Google and visitors understand the photos

On your website:

- **Name files clearly** before uploading: "roof-replacement-tulsa-before.jpg," not "IMG_4821.jpg."
- **Write alt text** that describes the photo, such as "New asphalt shingle roof on a two-story home in Tulsa."
- **Compress images** so pages stay fast on phones. Slow pages lose visitors; our guide to [Core Web Vitals](/blog/core-web-vitals-small-business-website) explains why.
- **Add a caption** with the service and town.

## A simple photo routine for your team

| When | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Arrival | The problem, wide and close up |
| During (optional) | Interesting steps, for bigger jobs |
| Finish | The same angles as the before shots |
| Weekly | Office picks the best pair for a Google post and the website |
