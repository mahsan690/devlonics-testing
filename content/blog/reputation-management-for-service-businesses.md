Reputation management for a service business comes down to four habits: ask every customer for a review, reply to every review, catch problems before they turn into one-star posts, and check the numbers once a month. None of it is complicated. The businesses that win are the ones that make it a routine instead of a one-off push.

Here's the playbook, with the 2026 numbers behind it.

## Why reputation matters more than ever for local businesses

[BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/) of US consumers shows how closely people read reviews before they call:

- **97%** read reviews for local businesses, and **41%** always do.
- **74%** only consider reviews from the last three months.
- **31%** will only use businesses rated **4.5 stars or higher**, up from 17% a year earlier.
- **47%** won't use a business with fewer than **20 reviews**.
- **45%** now use AI tools like ChatGPT when researching local businesses.

Reviews also feed Google's local ranking. Google says it ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence, and that [review count and rating factor into prominence](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091). So your reputation decides both whether you show up and whether people pick you once you do.

## Habit 1: Ask every customer, every time

The most common reputation problem isn't bad reviews. It's too few recent ones. A business that finishes 60 jobs a month but asks only when someone remembers will look quieter than a smaller competitor with a system.

What works:

- **Ask at the moment of relief:** when the AC is blowing cold, the leak is fixed or the floor is finished.
- **Send a direct link** that opens the review box, by text, so it takes ten seconds.
- **Automate the follow-up:** one reminder a few days later if they haven't left one.
- **Ask everyone.** Google's [rating manipulation policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) prohibits selectively asking only happy customers for reviews, the practice often called review gating.

## Habit 2: Reply to every review

BrightLocal found **89%** of consumers expect business owners to respond to reviews, and **50%** are put off by generic replies. Your replies are marketing: they're read by every future customer who checks you out.

**For positive reviews,** thank the customer by name and mention something specific they said: the technician, the job, the speed. Two sentences is plenty.

**For negative reviews,** follow a simple pattern:

1. Thank them for the feedback, without sarcasm.
2. Acknowledge the specific problem. Don't argue the details in public.
3. Say what you'll do, and give a name and number to contact.
4. If you fix it, it's fine to ask whether they'd update the review. Don't offer anything in return.

A calm, specific reply often does more for your reputation than the original review costs you, because it shows how you behave when something goes wrong.

## Habit 3: Catch problems before they're posted

The best negative review is the one that never gets written. Two simple systems help:

- **A quick private check-in after each job,** such as "How did we do? Reply 1 to 5." Low scores alert the owner, who calls the customer the same day.
- **Alerts for new reviews** on Google, Yelp and Facebook, so nothing sits unanswered for weeks.

One rule: the check-in must not decide who gets the review link. Everyone still gets asked for a public review, or it becomes review gating.

## Habit 4: Deal with fake or unfair reviews properly

Some reviews really do break the rules: a competitor, an ex-employee, someone who was never a customer, or spam. Google lets you [report a review from your Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4596773) when it violates policy.

What doesn't work: arguing in the reply, threatening the reviewer, or asking friends to bury it with five-star reviews. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews, final in 2024, bans [suppressing negative reviews through threats and buying reviews of a particular sentiment](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials), with civil penalties for knowing violations. Our [guide to the FTC review rule](/blog/ftc-fake-review-rule-small-business) covers what's allowed.

## Beyond Google: where else your reputation lives

Google gets most of the attention because it drives map rankings and calls, but customers also check:

- **Yelp and Facebook,** especially for cleaning, landscaping and home care.
- **BBB, Angi, Houzz** and trade directories, depending on your industry.
- **AI assistants,** which summarize what they find across those sources. Consistent details and recent reviews everywhere help you get described accurately.

You don't need a big presence on every site. Claim your profiles, keep the details identical and reply where your customers actually post.

## What to measure each month

Keep it to four numbers:

| Measure | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| New reviews this month | Recency is what customers look for first |
| Average rating (last 90 days) | Shows the trend, not just the lifetime score |
| Response rate | Should be 100% |
| Calls from your Google profile | The outcome reputation is meant to drive |
